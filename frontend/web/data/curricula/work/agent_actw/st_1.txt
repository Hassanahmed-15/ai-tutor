U|Unit 1: Exploring One-Variable Data and Collecting Data
T|1.1 Introducing Statistics: What Can We Learn from Data?
S|Parts of a statistical study
1.1.A|Identify the components of a statistical study
1.1.A.1|A statistical study collects sample data to answer a question about a larger population
1.1.A.2|Studies are needed when surveying the whole population is too hard
1.1.A.3|A datum is one piece of information; many data form a data set
1.1.A.4|A population is everything of interest; its size is N
1.1.A.5|A sample is a subset of the population; its size is n
1.1.A.6|Relate every part of a study and its results to the real-world context ("in context")
S|Investigative questions
1.1.B|Determine an investigative question within a statistical study
1.1.B.1|A question has a defined purpose and is not changed after seeing results
1.1.B.2|Pose the question so the needed data can be collected and analysed
T|1.2 Variables
S|Units, variables, parameters and statistics
1.2.A|Identify observational units, variables, parameters and statistics
1.2.A.1|An observational unit is an item or individual from which data are collected
1.2.A.2|A variable is a characteristic that can differ between units
1.2.A.3|Numerical and categorical data, including images, sounds and text, can carry information
1.2.A.4|A parameter summarises a variable for a population
1.2.A.5|A statistic summarises a variable for a sample and is used to infer the parameter
S|Types of variables
1.2.B|Identify types of variables
1.2.B.1|Categorical variables take category names or group labels
1.2.B.2|Quantitative variables take measured or counted numerical values, usually with units
1.2.C|Identify types of quantitative variables
1.2.C.1|Discrete variables take countable values
1.2.C.2|Continuous variables take any value within an interval

T|1.7 Summary Statistics for One Quantitative Variable
S|Center and position
1.7.A|Calculate measures of center and position
1.7.A.1|Mean and median are the common measures of center
1.7.A.2|The mean is the sum of values divided by their count
1.7.A.3|The median is the middle value of the ordered data
1.7.A.4|Minimum and maximum are the smallest and largest values
1.7.A.5|Quartiles Q1, Q2, Q3 split the ordered data into quarters
1.7.A.6|The pth percentile has p percent of values at or below it
S|Variability
1.7.B|Calculate measures of variability
1.7.B.1|Range, IQR and standard deviation measure spread
1.7.B.2|Range is maximum minus minimum
1.7.B.3|IQR is Q3 minus Q1
1.7.B.4|Standard deviation is a typical distance from the mean; its square is the variance
S|Units, outliers and comparison
1.7.C|Calculate effects of different units of measurement
1.7.C.1|Changing measurement units changes the statistics
1.7.D|Calculate outliers
1.7.D.1|Flag outliers by the 1.5 times IQR rule or by 2 standard deviations from the mean
1.7.E|Compare summary statistics across samples
1.7.E.1|Compare center, variability, shape and outliers between independent samples
S|Choosing a summary statistic
1.7.F|Justify the choice of summary statistic
1.7.F.1|Median and IQR are resistant to outliers; mean, range and standard deviation are not
1.7.F.2|Summary statistics can justify claims in context

1.8.B|Describe graphs using the mean-median relationship

T|1.10 The Investigative Question Revisited and Data Collection
S|Components of an investigative question
1.10.A|Determine the components of an investigative question
1.10.A.1|The first part guides data collection and names the variables
1.10.A.2|The second part guides the analysis, naming the parameter and the alternative direction or estimation goal
1.10.A.3|The third part states the population and any cause-and-effect conclusion
S|Census, experiments and observational studies
1.10.B|Identify a census
1.10.B.1|A census records information from every unit in the population
1.10.C|Identify an experiment
1.10.C.1|An experiment assigns treatments to experimental units
1.10.C.2|The experimental unit is the unit receiving the treatment
1.10.C.3|Explanatory variables (factors) have levels that are the treatments
1.10.C.4|The response variable is measured after treatment
1.10.D|Identify an observational study
1.10.D.1|Observational studies do not impose treatments
1.10.D.2|Prospective studies gather data now and in the future
1.10.D.3|Retrospective studies gather past data
1.10.D.4|A survey asks humans a standard set of questions
1.10.D.5|A confounding variable linked to both explanatory and response variables offers an alternative explanation
S|Justifying generalizations
1.10.E|Justify the appropriateness of generalizations
1.10.E.1|A random sample is chosen by a random mechanism
1.10.E.2|Random selection allows generalising to the whole population
1.10.E.3|Volunteer or deliberately chosen samples are not random
1.10.E.4|Without random selection generalise only to similar individuals
T|1.11 Random Sampling
S|Sampling methods
1.11.A|Identify a sampling method from a study description
1.11.A.1|Sampling without replacement selects each unit at most once
1.11.A.2|Sampling with replacement can select a unit more than once
1.11.A.3|In a simple random sample every sample of size n is equally likely
1.11.A.4|A stratified sample takes a simple random sample from each stratum
1.11.A.5|A cluster sample randomly selects clusters and surveys everyone in them
1.11.A.6|A systematic sample uses a random start and fixed interval
1.11.B|Justify the appropriateness of a sampling method
1.11.B.1|Each method suits different populations and questions
T|1.12 Potential Problems with Sampling
S|Sources of bias
1.12.A|Identify potential sources of bias in sampling
1.12.A.1|Bias is systematic error pushing a statistic consistently high or low
1.12.A.2|Voluntary response bias arises when the sample is all volunteers
1.12.A.3|Undercoverage bias arises when part of the population is missed
1.12.A.4|Nonresponse bias arises when chosen individuals do not respond
1.12.A.5|Response bias arises from confusing or leading questions or self-reports
1.12.A.6|Nonrandom methods such as convenience samples risk bias
T|1.13 Experimental Design
S|Elements of a good experiment
1.13.A|Identify elements of a well-designed experiment
1.13.A.1|Include comparison of treatments, random assignment, replication and direct control
1.13.A.2|A control group provides a comparison, sometimes with a placebo
1.13.A.3|The placebo effect is the response to a placebo compared with no treatment
1.13.A.4|In a single-blind experiment only participants or only the research team know the treatments
1.13.A.5|In a double-blind experiment neither group knows the treatments
1.13.A.6|An extraneous variable affects the response but is not studied
S|Random assignment, confounding, replication
1.13.A.7|Random assignment makes treatment groups similar on extraneous variables
1.13.A.8|A confounding variable is tangled with the explanatory variable; good design reduces it
1.13.A.9|Replication means more than one unit per treatment
1.13.A.10|Direct control holds extraneous settings the same across units
S|Experimental designs
1.13.B|Identify experimental designs
1.13.B.1|In a completely randomized design treatments are assigned entirely at random
1.13.B.2|A randomized block design groups similar units into blocks and randomises within each
1.13.B.3|Blocking separates blocking-variable variation to compare treatments more precisely
1.13.B.4|A matched pairs design is a two-treatment block design on pairs or on repeated units
1.13.C|Justify an experimental design
1.13.C.1|The best design depends on goals, population, sample and variables
1.13.D|Justify conclusions from an experiment
1.13.D.1|Random assignment permits cause-and-effect conclusions
1.13.D.2|When random selection is impractical or unethical, volunteers represent similar units
