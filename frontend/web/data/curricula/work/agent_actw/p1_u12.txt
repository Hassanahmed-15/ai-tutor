U|Unit 2: Force and Translational Dynamics

S|Systems and their interactions
2.1.A|Describe the properties and interactions of a system
2.1.A.1|System properties come from the interactions of objects inside it
2.1.A.2|A system can be treated as one object when its internal details do not matter

2.1.A.4|Individual objects in a system may behave differently from the system as a whole
2.1.A.5|The internal structure of a system affects how it must be analysed

S|Forces as interactions
2.2.A|Describe a force as an interaction between two objects or systems

T|2.3 Newton's Third Law
S|Newton's third law and tension
2.3.A|Describe an interaction using Newton's third law and paired forces
2.3.A.1|The two objects in an interaction exert equal and opposite forces on each other
2.3.A.2|Internal forces do not change the motion of a system's center of mass
2.3.A.3|Tension is the net result of forces between segments of a string under external force
T|2.4 Newton's First Law
S|Constant velocity and equilibrium
2.4.A|Describe the conditions under which a system's velocity stays constant
2.4.A.1|Net force is the vector sum of all forces on the system
2.4.A.2|Translational equilibrium means the net force is zero
2.4.A.3|With zero net force, velocity stays constant (Newton's first law)
2.4.A.4|Forces balanced in one dimension may be unbalanced in another
2.4.A.5|An inertial frame is one where Newton's first law holds
T|2.5 Newton's Second Law
S|Newton's second law
2.5.A|Describe the conditions under which a system's velocity changes
2.5.A.1|Unbalanced forces mean a nonzero net force
2.5.A.2|Acceleration is proportional to net force and in the same direction (Newton's second law)
2.5.A.3|Velocity of the center of mass changes only with a nonzero net external force
T|2.6 Gravitational Force
S|Universal gravitation and fields
2.6.A|Describe the gravitational interaction between two objects with mass
2.6.A.1|Gravitational force is proportional to both masses and inversely to distance squared; attractive, along the line of centers
2.6.A.2|A field models a noncontact force; gravitational field strength is force per unit mass
2.6.A.3|Weight is the gravitational force from an astronomical body on a small nearby object
S|Constant gravity near Earth
2.6.B|Describe when the gravitational force can be treated as constant
2.6.B.1|Gravity is constant when it barely changes over the displacement
2.6.B.2|Near Earth's surface, g is about 10 N/kg
S|Apparent weight
2.6.C|Describe when apparent weight differs from the gravitational force
2.6.C.1|Apparent weight is the magnitude of the normal force on the system
2.6.C.2|In an accelerating system, apparent weight differs from gravitational force
2.6.C.3|A system appears weightless when gravity is the only force on it
2.6.C.4|The equivalence principle: an observer in a noninertial frame cannot tell apparent weight from gravity
S|Inertial and gravitational mass
2.6.D|Describe inertial and gravitational mass
2.6.D.1|Inertial mass measures resistance to changes in motion
2.6.D.2|Gravitational mass relates to the gravitational attraction between systems
2.6.D.3|Inertial and gravitational mass have been experimentally found to be equal

S|Kinetic friction
2.7.A|Describe kinetic friction between two surfaces
2.7.A.1|Kinetic friction acts when surfaces slide, opposing relative motion and independent of contact area
2.7.A.2|Kinetic friction equals the coefficient of kinetic friction times the normal force; the normal force is perpendicular to the surface
S|Static friction
2.7.B|Describe static friction between two surfaces
2.7.B.1|Static friction can act between surfaces not moving relative to each other
2.7.B.2|Static friction adjusts to prevent slipping, up to a maximum value
2.7.B.3|The static coefficient is typically greater than the kinetic coefficient
